Green Efficiency Analysis
Measurement Methodology
The experiments used three C programs to examine execution time under a fixed workload. Each was compiled with gcc -Wall -Wextra -Werror -pedantic -std=gnu89. The programs used clock() and CLOCKS_PER_SEC to report elapsed CPU time in seconds. This measures processor time consumed by the program, not electrical energy or necessarily wall-clock duration.

For baseline_loop.c, the fixed workload was 100,000,000 iterations. Three consecutive runs reported 0.093925, 0.093598, and 0.093284 seconds, giving an average of approximately 0.093602 seconds. For comparison_algorithms.c, three runs captured timings for both the naive and single-pass implementations. Finally, instrumentation_lab.c measured the build, process, and reduction stages individually, as well as the total CPU time around those stages. Its recorded output was one execution, so the phase results represent a snapshot rather than a multi-run average.
Observed Performance Differences
The naive algorithm took 1.818218, 1.822476, and 2.106307 seconds, averaging 1.915667 seconds. The single-pass algorithm took 0.000100, 0.000103, and 0.000108 seconds, averaging 0.000103667 seconds before rounding. Dividing the averages gives a measured runtime ratio of approximately 18,479:1. The recorded times therefore show a substantial execution-time difference between these particular implementations.

However, their results were not equal: the naive function returned 625025000, whereas the single-pass function returned 25000. The naive implementation repeatedly counts even entries in overlapping ranges, while the single-pass implementation counts each even entry once. Consequently, this is not a controlled comparison of two equivalent solutions to exactly the same computation. The result difference is essential when interpreting the speed ratio.

The instrumented program reported 0.000741 seconds TOTAL, with 0.000293 seconds BUILD_DATA, 0.000309 seconds PROCESS, and 0.000135 seconds REDUCE. PROCESS was the slowest measured phase in that run, followed by BUILD_DATA and REDUCE. The three phase times total 0.000737 seconds, just below the reported total; the remaining 0.000004 seconds is consistent with the measurement boundaries and timing overhead.
Relation Between Runtime and Energy Consumption
Execution time is relevant to energy efficiency because an active computation uses computing resources over time. The measurements show that the single-pass implementation consumed far less measured CPU time than the naive implementation. This makes it a promising candidate for lower computational resource use in this test, but energy savings cannot be quantified from these results. None of the programs measured electrical power, joules, or hardware energy counters. A faster runtime alone does not establish an exact reduction in energy consumption, especially when the implementations compute different results.
Limitations of the Experiment
The tests were performed with fixed input sizes and only three timing runs for the baseline and algorithm comparison; the instrumented phases have only one recorded run. Timing variability is visible: the naive implementation ranged from 1.818218 to 2.106307 seconds. Very short single-pass and phase measurements are also sensitive to timer resolution and execution conditions. The experiments provide no direct power measurements, and the two comparison functions do not produce equivalent outputs. These limitations restrict conclusions about general performance and energy efficiency.
Practical Engineering Takeaway
Measure before optimizing, retain the raw outputs, and compare both runtime and correctness. The observed phase timings identify PROCESS as the first stage worth investigating in instrumentation_lab.c, but additional runs would be needed to confirm that ranking. The algorithm comparison demonstrates a large CPU-time gap, while also showing why performance numbers must not be separated from what the code actually computes. For stronger green-efficiency claims, future tests should compare equivalent outputs across repeated runs and measure energy directly.
